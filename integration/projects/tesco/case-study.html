Nithin Narayanan / Supply chain case study

Tesco-Inspired Inventory Optimisation

A simulated UK grocery retail case study using Python, SQL and an interactive dashboard.

Independent portfolio project using synthetic data. Not affiliated with or endorsed by Tesco. Results are simulated and do not represent Tesco’s actual performance.

The question

How can a grocery retailer improve availability without tying up unnecessary cash in inventory or letting short-life products expire? Inspired by my exposure to retail operations, I built an independent model to explore that question.

The experiment

60 SKUs, three fictional stores and 365 days of seeded synthetic demand. Categories include dairy, bakery, produce, beverages, frozen goods and household essentials. January–September fits policy parameters; October–December is an untouched 92-day evaluation window.

Both policies start with identical stock and no pipeline. Baseline reviews weekly and orders to mean demand × (mean lead time + seven days). Proposed reviews daily, adds variability-based safety stock and caps orders for perishables. Both fulfil orders by first expiry date.

What the simulation found

  • Availability: unit fill rate increased from 83.4% to 99.6%; lost units fell from 33,908 to 886.
  • Working capital: average inventory at cost fell from £32,012 to £27,231 (14.9%).
  • Expiry: waste fell from 58,445 to 7,987 units.
  • Trade-off: SKU-store orders rose from 2,520 to 12,027; ordering cost rose from £5,040 to £24,054.
  • Modelled cost: holding, ordering, expiry and lost contribution cost totalled £220,269 under baseline and £50,619 under the proposed policy.

Why the improvement is so large

The baseline intentionally uses one simple weekly policy for every product. That is particularly unsuitable for bakery products with three-day shelf lives. The results demonstrate the value of matching replenishment to shelf life; they do not establish that Tesco or another real retailer could achieve comparable savings. The more frequent orders also create operational workload that this model values only through an illustrative £2 order cost.

Methods

Training ABC classes use cumulative demand value with 80% and 95% boundaries. Safety stock = z × √[(mean lead + 1) × demand variance + mean demand² × lead variance]. The reorder point covers mean demand during lead time plus one review day and safety stock. Normal approximations are a planning heuristic; target z values do not guarantee achieved fill rate.

Fixed-origin weekday-mean forecasting achieved 32.8% WAPE versus 42.0% for seasonal naive on Q4. These are benchmarks; the replenishment policy uses a constant training mean, not those weekday forecasts. Annual descriptive consumption values use actual fulfilled synthetic baseline units × cost and remain separate from policy fitting.

Practical recommendations

  • Review fresh categories daily and constrain stock to shelf life; do not copy a weekly household-goods policy into bakery.
  • Prioritise high-value A products, but monitor realised availability rather than assuming a z-score delivers a service target.
  • Evaluate delivery consolidation and minimum order quantities before accepting the increased ordering workload.
  • Pilot with actual demand, stockout and batch-age data before setting live reorder points.

Limitations and next steps

Demand is fully visible in this synthetic experiment; real lost demand needs estimation. No store substitution, capacity, pack sizes, minimum quantities, correlated supplier disruptions or consolidated truck costs are modelled. Batch shelf life starts at receipt. Inventory position does not anticipate expiry in transit. The evaluation resets stock at October 1 rather than continuing the annual baseline. End-period inventory and outstanding orders receive no terminal valuation. Forecasts are not promotion-aware. A stronger next step is a rolling backtest against an existing daily-review policy, with realistic supplier costs and shelf-life constraints.

Tools and evidence

Python, pandas, NumPy, SQLite and dependency-free HTML/CSS/JavaScript. Reproducible generation, inventory reconciliation, causality checks, SQL queries and dashboard reconciliation are included in the source package.
